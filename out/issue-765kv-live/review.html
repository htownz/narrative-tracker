India’s Next #PowerBoom Is About Wires, Storage & the 8 PM Problem India has entered a new phase of the electricity cycle. The country has added a huge amount of renewable generation, but the problem is increasingly not about how much electricity India can generate. It is about where that electricity is available and when it is available. Solar power is abundant during the day, but disappears after sunset, while evening demand keeps rising. In September 2026, India recorded a 544 MU power shortfall, with the worst evening gap reaching around 7.7 GW. At the same time, renewable power was being curtailed because transmission capacity was not ready. This is where the government’s ₹1.86 lakh crore Green Energy Corridor Phase-III (GEC-III) becomes important. The plan allocates around ₹1.36 lakh crore for intra-state transmission infrastructure and ₹50,000 crore for 50 GWh of battery storage. It also targets 51,126 circuit-km of new transmission lines, 2,28,903 MVA of substation capacity and evacuation of up to 135 GW of renewable power. In simple language, India is building the electricity equivalent of new highways and warehouses: wires to move power and batteries to store it for later use. This policy creates several structural changes. First, India is shifting from “generate more power” to “move and time the power.” Second, transmission is becoming a precondition for renewable development rather than something built after the power plant. Third, state transmission networks are opening up to private companies through Tariff-Based Competitive Bidding (TBCB), where the winner can build, own and operate the transmission asset for the long term. Fourth, batteries are becoming grid infrastructure, not just an EV or electronics product. India had only about 1.8 GWh of grid battery capacity by March 2026, while the CEA sees a requirement of more than 236 GWh by 2031-32. The value chain is also becoming much larger. At the raw-material level, the industry needs aluminium, copper, CRGO electrical steel and other materials. These become conductors, winding wires, transformer cores, bushings and insulators. Those components then go into large transformers, reactors, switchgear, GIS and HVDC systems. These are installed through transmission and substation EPC companies, while companies winning transmission auctions ultimately own the assets and earn long-term tariffs. In parallel, another chain is developing from battery cells → packs → BESS containers → inverters/software → storage owners. Coal, hydro and pumped storage remain important as the bridge for evening power until batteries scale sufficiently. The biggest beneficiaries are therefore spread across several layers. #PowerGrid, #AdaniEnergySolutions and #TataPower benefit from transmission ownership. #HitachiEnergyIndia, #CGPower, #SiemensEnergyIndia, #GEVernovaIndia, #BHEL and #QualityPower are exposed to high-voltage and HVDC equipment, where the technology and qualification barriers are much higher. #AparIndustries, #Polycab, #KEI and #UniversalCables benefit from new transmission lines and reconductoring. #LT, #KECInternational, #KalpataruProjects, #TechnoElectric, #Transrail, #Bajel, #Skipper and #VikranEngineering benefit from transmission and substation construction. On the storage side, #RelianceIndustries, #WaareeEnergies, #ExideIndustries and #AmaraRaja are building capabilities in the battery ecosystem. while #JSWEnergy, #NLCIndia, #AdaniGreen and #NTPCGreen are potential beneficiaries as storage owners. But the most important point for investors is that a structural industry does not mean every company will have structural profits. The deepest moats appear to be in 765 kV equipment, HVDC and transmission ownership. EPC companies, tower makers, mid-sized transformer manufacturers and battery assemblers can see strong growth, but they are much more exposed to bidding pressure, working capital, capacity additions and margin normalisation. Transformer capacity, for example, is expected to rise sharply from around 375 GVA in FY25 to roughly 690 GVA by FY28, which could eventually reduce today’s shortage-driven margins. For an investor, the biggest lesson is to follow the cash, not the headline. A GEC-III approval is not the same as an order, an L1 position is not the same as a signed contract, and a large order book is not valuable if receivables keep rising. Watch order quality, working capital, operating cash flow, construction-period accounting, promoter behaviour, transformer capacity versus demand, battery economics and the pace of actual GEC-III tenders. The big picture is simple: India is building not just more power plants, but an entirely larger electricity network around them. Solar and wind create electricity, transmission moves it, batteries shift it into the evening, and firm power fills the remaining gaps. That is why wires, transformers, HVDC, grid equipment and storage could remain major infrastructure themes for years. The real investment question is not whether the grid rebuild is happening; it is which companies can turn that structural demand into durable free cash flow without relying on temporary shortage margins.open ↗
