Did some digging into $NUAI. It reads more like a 2028 story to me. The valuation math is easy to get excited about until you look at what happened with $CIFR and $WULF. A major lease can re-rate the stock immediately. Then the market starts underwriting what actually matters: capex, financing, dilution, tenant credit, delivery timing and when rent actually starts. And $CIFR is probably the best example. Cipher signed its first major HPC lease with Fluidstack in September 2025: 168 MW, roughly $3B of contracted revenue, with Google backstopping $1.4B of Fluidstack's obligations. It then followed that with a 15-year ~$5.5B direct AWS lease and additional contracted capacity. Cipher eventually had something NUAI doesn't have today: multiple executed leases, hyperscaler validation, access to billions in project capital and an established data center development platform. Yet after trading around $30 at its highs, $CIFR eventually came back toward the ~$14 area—roughly around where it was when that first major Fluidstack/Google lease was announced. That doesn't mean the leases were bad. They materially improved CIFR's fundamentals. The point is that even after the market gets the lease headlines it wants, eventually it comes back to the economics. NUAI hasn't even reached that point yet. Phase 1 is 200–207 MW supplied by Vistra's Luminant subsidiary at the Odessa site. The 20-year PPA was signed September 18, with power targeted for Q3 2027. The site itself is real. Odessa P&Z approved the 63.42-acre Phase 1 plat 4–0, and NUAI already had grading-related approvals in place. The power, however, is not online yet. The PPA still has conditions precedent that have to be satisfied by December 31, 2027, including the agreement covering the Phase 1 substation and related equipment. NUAI is also required to provide a $116M letter of credit, originally due within 15 business days of signing, plus up to another $82.8M of security by the delivery date. That's potentially $198.8M of credit support associated with getting Phase 1 power delivered. As of this writing, I haven't found public confirmation that the $116M LC has been posted. And there is still no binding tenant. NUAI says it is negotiating with multiple potential tenants, including what it describes as one of the world's largest AI infrastructure consumers. But even if an LOI gets announced, an LOI isn't a lease. That distinction matters. And this is where I think comparisons to $CIFR and $WULF need context. Yes, established developers have signed major AI/hyperscale leases before their campuses were fully energized. But I haven't found a clean comp for a company in NUAI's position. NUAI only pivoted into dedicated data center development in the second half of 2025. TCDC is its first hyperscale campus. It doesn't have CIFR's execution history, multiple completed financings or existing hyperscaler relationships. That doesn't mean NUAI can't sign a hyperscaler before energization. But the burden of proof is different. A Microsoft, Amazon or Google signing today isn't simply reserving 200 MW of future capacity. They're underwriting NUAI and its development partners successfully posting the required credit support, completing the substation transaction, raising billions in project financing, constructing the campus and delivering that capacity on schedule. Maybe a hyperscaler signs before energization. But every milestone NUAI clears—the $116M LC, substation agreement, financing, permits and construction progress—makes the project easier for an investment-grade tenant to underwrite. If NUAI lands Microsoft, Amazon, Google or another investment-grade tenant before those milestones, that's a major de-risking event and I'd adjust the thesis accordingly. Until then, I don't think CIFR signing AWS early proves NUAI can automatically do the same. And without a binding lease or sufficiently creditworthy development structure, financing a multi-billion-dollar shell becomes much harder. That's the part of the valuation people skip. Recent powered-shell/colocation transactions roughly support annual economics around $1.7–2.4M per critical IT MW. Use $1.8M/MW as a conservative illustrative assumption for Phase 1. 200 MW × $1.8M = roughly $360M of annual rent. That's: $3.6B over 10 years. $5.4B over 15 years. Sounds huge. But NUAI doesn't keep $360M. The lease rate determines the size of the pie. The financing structure determines NUAI's slice. And the fully diluted share count determines each shareholder's slice of NUAI. Vistra receives a 5% non-voting interest in the project company associated with the PPA-powered portion once delivery begins. NUAI management has also discussed an illustrative structure involving roughly a 50/50 development JV financed with approximately 80% project debt and 20% equity. That helps solve the capital problem. But it also means sharing the economics. Take $360M of headline annual rent. A roughly 50% interest gets you closer to $180M of attributable rent before operating expenses, interest, debt amortization, development economics and other costs. The lender gets paid first. NUAI common shareholders own the residual, not the headline rent. And tenant quality changes the value of that residual. A 15–20 year lease with Microsoft, Amazon or Google can support cheaper debt and a higher valuation multiple. A shorter lease with a weaker-credit neocloud can mean more expensive financing, more required equity and a lower multiple. Same MW. Same building. Very different equity value. If NUAI has to fund a $200–400M equity requirement primarily by selling stock at $6, that's roughly 33–67M new shares. Same project. Completely different per-share outcome. Best case, NUAI lands a strong investment-grade tenant earlier, project financing follows, construction progresses on schedule, power arrives around late 2027 and initial rent begins in 2028. A well-financed ~50% interest in 200 MW leased long term to Microsoft, Amazon, Google or another investment-grade counterparty can justify $10–$13 and potentially more. Every arrow matters. The site is real. The PPA is real. The opportunity is real. But Q3 2027 is a power target—not a 200 MW data center full of paying customers. For me, $10–$13 makes more sense as a successfully executed Phase 1 valuation than something I automatically assign today.
