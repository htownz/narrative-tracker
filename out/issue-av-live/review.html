Dear Mr. Anderson, Tesla never ignored physics. A 2028 Cadillac Escalade IQ will launch eyes-off highway driving with cameras, radar and lidar all working together. As I understand it, your case rests on four claims. Lidar helps camera systems learn faster, cameras can't see through dense fog, cameras can't measure instantaneous velocity the way radar can, and radar is so cheap that removing it makes no sense. I agree that physics is real, and nobody at Tesla is pretending otherwise. You're right, and Tesla agrees so strongly that it built an entire training pipeline around that idea. I like to think of lidar as a tutor holding a perfect answer key. It measures distance directly by timing laser pulses, so it can grade every guess a camera network makes about how far away something is. Tesla has designed a system where a special test fleet carries radar and lidar alongside the regular cameras. Every reading is synchronized, so the precise depth and speed from the expensive sensors get attached automatically to the objects the cameras see. The labeling even uses hindsight. When two cars blur into one blob early in a clip and separate later, the training servers rewind the footage and use the clear moment to label the confusing one correctly. Tesla applies the same teacher to 3D space itself. Its occupancy network learns from lidar-backed ground truth to predict, for every small cube of space around the car, how far that cube sits from the nearest surface. That's how plain cameras learn to sketch a sloped curb or a curved bumper smoothly instead of as a pile of blocky Lego bricks. Even the grading got faster. Tesla designed a cross-labeling trick that draws a box around a car in a flat photo, then projects that box into the laser point cloud as a pyramid of vision, so the software only searches that slice instead of the whole 3D world. You probably know DeepScale, the startup Tesla acquired in 2019. It was already working out in 2016 how to train cheap sensors to mimic premium lidar data, and Tesla is still pursuing that work today. Here's the step where our views split. The student is even learning to write its own answer key. Co-training requires lidar in the classroom, not in every car on the road. With respect, GM's plan keeps the teacher riding along forever. ⚡ ON SPEED, CAMERAS ARE QUICKER THAN THEY LOOK Your sharpest point is about speed. Radar can measure how fast something is moving in a single snapshot, and a camera can't. Radar's trick is the Doppler effect, the same physics that makes an ambulance siren sound higher as it rushes toward you and lower as it drives away. Radar reads that pitch shift in its own radio echo to measure closing speed instantly. A camera has to work speed out from motion. It compares where an object sits in one frame with where it sits in the next, much like you judge a thrown ball by watching it fly. That sounds slower, but the gap is tiny. Tesla's own designs describe cameras running at rates such as 36 frames per second, which puts a fresh frame in front of the network about every 28 milliseconds, quicker than a single blink. The physics also cuts both ways. Doppler only measures motion toward or away from the sensor, so a car crossing your path sideways at an intersection produces little to no Doppler shift. To catch that sideways motion, a radar has to track the car across several scans, which is exactly what a camera does with frames. On that kind of motion, the "instantaneous" advantage disappears. Radar has another well-known weak spot. Owner's manuals for radar-based cruise control have long warned that the system may not brake for a stopped vehicle, partly because a parked car, an overhead sign and a bridge can all look like the same motionless metal. Now here's what Tesla has built since you left. Tesla has designed its vision network to output a full 3D velocity vector for every object, covering speed, direction and even height. It learned that skill from the radar on its test fleet, which turns ordinary cameras into something that behaves like a Doppler radar made of pixels. Tesla's virtual camera invention goes further by measuring speed in two different ways. Cars are tracked relative to the moving Tesla itself, while pedestrians are tracked against the fixed ground, so the system knows a person's true walking path even while the car accelerates or turns. When things get fast, the network speeds up its memory. A frame selector pulls video snapshots more rapidly as speeds rise and blends them with the car's own acceleration and yaw rate, which is how quickly the car is rotating, to build a smooth picture of motion over time. There's also a dedicated sentinel for the car directly ahead. A super-narrow model watches only the closest vehicle in the lane through the narrowest forward lens and keeps estimating its distance, speed and acceleration, which is exactly what you need to brake hard and early. It can't read lane paint, a traffic light, a speed limit sign or a flagger's hand signal, so even a car loaded with radar still leans on its cameras when the world turns gray. The real question is whether the car knows when its vision is degraded, and whether it behaves like a careful driver when it is. This is where Tesla's visibility invention comes in.
