The Republican national school voucher program drains resources from public schools and benefits the wealthy.
factcheckableagainstmisleading
why
The claim is a sweeping two-part judgment. The records support parts of it, but not as worded. - "National" program: The federal program is a tax-credit scholarship in the 2025 reconciliation law. It starts in January 2027. Donors get a credit of up to $1,700, and scholarship eligibility is limited to students in households at or below 300% of area median income . The statute also lets the scholarships fund public school expenses . Texas's own ESA/TEFA program is a separate state program, created by SB 2, and it does not come from the federal law. - "Benefits the wealthy": The Texas data mostly cut against this. The Comptroller's demographic report shows about 80% of funded students come from households below 200% of the federal poverty level . Critics have a different point: 57% of an early set of accepted students were already in private school or homeschool , and later data put the share of grade 1–12 awardees at about 70%. Those figures show the money often goes to families who had already left public schools, not that it goes to wealthy families. Ted Cruz's legislation creates scholarships funded by private donations, not public money.
factcheckableformisleading
why
Cruz's provision, enacted in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, funds scholarships through donations to nonprofit scholarship-granting organizations, so "donations" is accurate. But the donors get a dollar-for-dollar federal tax credit of up to $1,700. Individual taxpayers can receive a dollar-for-dollar federal income tax credit of up to $1,700 annually (Texas AFT, citing the Treasury/IRS rules; Gov. Abbott's release says the same). Brookings notes this is a credit, not a deduction, so it really does offset the "donation" . The scholarship money is therefore private in form, but donors pay less federal tax by the same amount, so the cost falls on the federal Treasury. The Houston Chronicle likewise describes the measure as a tax break for donors. Calling it "not public money" leaves that out, and it is also separate from Texas's state-funded ESA program (SB 2, $1 billion), which does use public funds. The claim is defensible on the narrow point that donors rather than the government write the checks to the scholarship groups. As worded, though, it is misleading. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Ted Cruz's school choice program does not divert any dollar from Arizona's public-school funding, so claims it defunds Arizona schools are false.
factcheckableformisleading
why
Ted Cruz's school choice program is the federal scholarship tax credit that he championed and that was enacted in H.R.1 ( Club for Growth ). It gives a dollar-for-dollar credit of up to $1,700 for donations to scholarship-granting organizations. Arizona has not opted in, and Gov. Hobbs vetoed the opt-in bill. KJZZ describes the credit as a federal program that wouldn't cost the state anything to take advantage of. The Fountain Hills Times reports that the new program affects federal rather than Arizona tax collections. So the narrow point that Cruz's federal credit takes no money from Arizona's school funding has some support. But the claim is misleading as worded. It treats Cruz's program as if it were Arizona's school choice program, and it is not. Arizona's universal private school voucher program is the state's own Empowerment Scholarship Accounts. That program is state-funded. ESAs are funded at 90% of the state's per-pupil base funding , and the Common Sense Institute says ESA costs will probably reach $1 billion this year.
